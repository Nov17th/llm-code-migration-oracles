\paragraph{Construct validity.} Classifying oracles and contexts requires judgment, for instance when a compiler serves both as a filter and as a reported metric. We mitigate this with a written codebook, by coding only oracles that judge the final output and by discussing hard cases until agreement. \new{Our codes also record what papers write rather than what they do. A paper that states no oracle limitation may still be aware of one, so the 10 papers in that row of Table~\ref{tab:limits} measure reporting rather than awareness. Short papers describe their evaluation in less detail, so the 7 in the corpus (Section~\ref{sec:overview}) may understate the oracles they use. The origin codes in Table~\ref{tab:origin} likewise depend on what papers describe. Two differential-testing papers do not say how some of their inputs were produced~\cite{kcobagent,recodeagent}, and TransAgent does not say where the expected outputs of its LLM-generated tests come from~\cite{transagent}. Our rule codes TransAgent as O-e; had its expected outputs come from running the original, O-e would count one paper fewer and O-f one more. Other limits come from what we chose to code. We did not code whether tests and comparisons with the original ran in the full environment or with substitutes such as mocks or emulators, so we cannot tell how many results rest on such substitutes. Our legacy flag does not cover C either, so every count for legacy migration depends on that choice (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}). The gaps in Figure~\ref{fig:compile-gap} rest on reported numbers, since we re-run no experiment. Each gap also depends on the configuration we plot (named in the figure), and some papers count outputs that no test covers as not equivalent~\cite{alphatrans,ptrtrans}. The gaps are therefore indicative rather than exact.}

\paragraph{Internal validity.} A keyword search can miss papers. \new{Of 40 papers that meet our criteria and that we knew beforehand, the string retrieved 39; the scan also found the other 11 included papers. We found these 40 papers in exploratory searches of the same proceedings and in related work. Papers found this way can share the vocabulary of the string, so the check is not fully independent of it. Recall may be lower for papers that describe a migration in other words, like the dependency-repair paper the string missed~\cite{fruntke}. Coverage is incomplete in three further ways: for 80 papers without an abstract, the string could be matched against the title only}; the \new{poster tracks} of \new{FSE~2026 and ASE~2026} had no paper list at the time of the scan; and 3 papers available only behind a paywall were excluded under E6. \new{Screening could exclude a paper on its title and abstract, whereas every inclusion required the full text (Figure~\ref{fig:flow}). An early exclusion therefore risks dropping a relevant paper, and the recall check does not cover this step. Clarification rules decided 36 candidates that the criteria left open (Table~\ref{tab:rules}). Different rules could move some of them across the boundary of the corpus, in particular the 16 that L-TASK excluded because translation was not their main goal. Extraction can misread or overlook details, so we drafted every code from the full text and re-checked every number in the disagreement analysis (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}) against the papers. Some papers were read in a preprint version, whose numbers may differ from the published one; the screening data mark them.}

\paragraph{External validity.} The map \new{covers} the main proceedings of ICSE, FSE and ASE from 2024 to 2026. \new{Co-located workshops and conferences with their own proceedings are out of scope, although they also cover migration and legacy systems. Journal papers (unless presented at the three conferences) and papers at other venues such as ICSME, SANER and ESEM fall outside the map too.\cmtpink{P70, Minor; DA, DR}{Also name ML and NLP venues (ACL, EMNLP, NeurIPS, ICLR), where much LLM translation work and many benchmarks appear, probably with more similarity metrics, so the 27\% similarity share likely understates the field. One line on the wholesale exclusion of patch backporting and porting papers (C056, C085, C187, C210, C241) would pre-empt a reviewer question, since porting between versions resembles VER.} As a result, it misses studies such as the closely related one at ESEM~\cite{polyhuman}. Two sources of evidence lie outside any map of peer-reviewed venues: preprints on arXiv (where research on LLM-based migration often appears first) and the oracles that companies use in migrations they do not report. The map may lag behind this fast-changing research, and it captures industrial practice only through the 9 papers in industry tracks. Our conclusions therefore describe published practice at the three conferences from 2024 to 2026 and hold most firmly for translation between languages, the subject of 33 of the 49 papers.}

\begin{added}
\paragraph{The pilot.} The pilot shows that oracles can disagree on one fixed set of translations, not how often this happens in general or how the oracles would rank elsewhere. With a 7B translator and an 8B judge quantized to 4 bits, it runs once at temperature~0 on 164 single-function problems translated from Python to Java. This setup limits how far the results generalize: a larger judge, another prompt, other language pairs, repository-scale code or several samples per task could change the rates in Table~\ref{tab:pilot-oracles}. The benchmark adds a second limit, since HumanEval-X has been public for several years and both models may have seen its problems or solutions during training. Such exposure could raise the pass rate and the CodeBLEU scores. A third limit lies in the Java tests shipped with the benchmark, against which we compare the other oracles: they miss up to 16\% of the seeded faults that compile (Section~\ref{sec:pilot-design}). We neither ran the Python originals on generated inputs (which would give more direct evidence of equivalence) nor checked by hand which side is right when an oracle disagrees with the tests (beyond the examples in Section~\ref{sec:pilot-results}). Some translations that pass the tests may therefore be wrong, and some errors we attribute to the other oracles could be the tests' own. A fourth limit is sample size: only 32 translations fail the tests, so the false-accept rates have wide 95\% Wilson intervals that overlap. They run from 31\% to 64\% for compilation, from 26\% to 58\% for CodeBLEU at 0.6 and from 34\% to 66\% for the judge. Likewise, the judge leads CodeBLEU at 0.6 by just 5 tasks in agreement with the tests (121 against 116).\cmtpink{P73, Minor; R1}{All oracles judge the same tasks, so overlap of marginal Wilson intervals is not the right criterion; report the discordant pairs for each pair of oracles with a paired interval (e.g. Newcombe), which stays descriptive. The rates over 164 and 132 and the 41\% carry no interval (about 8 points either way at n = 164).}
\end{added}

\paragraph{Construct validity.} Classifying oracles and contexts requires judgment, for instance when a compiler serves both as a filter and as a reported metric. We mitigate this with a written codebook, by coding only oracles that judge the final output, and by discussing hard cases until agreement. \new{Some codes record what papers write rather than what they do. A paper that states no oracle limitation may still be aware of one, so the 10 papers in that row of Table~\ref{tab:limits} measure reporting, not awareness. The origin codes in Table~\ref{tab:origin} depend on how fully papers describe their tests and inputs, which is why two differential-testing papers fall under ``not described''. We take every reported number as published, without re-running any experiment, so the gaps in Figure~\ref{fig:compile-gap} compare \new{reported configurations. The configuration we plot for each paper (named in the figure) affects the size of its gap. \new{Some behavioral rates also count outputs that no test covers as failures~\cite{alphatrans,ptrtrans}, and ReCodeAgent's is a rate over tests rather than over outputs~\cite{recodeagent}, so the gaps are indicative rather than exact}}. Journal-first papers are counted under the conference and year that presented them.}

\paragraph{Internal validity.} A keyword search can miss papers. Against papers we knew in advance, the string retrieved 39 of 40. \new{These 40 papers come from exploratory searches of the same proceedings, partly with similar terms, so they are not independent of the string; recall may be lower for papers that describe a migration in other words, like the dependency-repair paper that the string missed~\cite{fruntke}.} \new{For 80 papers without an abstract, the keyword string could be matched against the title only}; the \new{poster tracks} of \new{FSE~2026 and ASE~2026} had no paper list at the time of the scan; and 3 papers available only behind a paywall were excluded under E6. \new{Exclusions could be decided on title and abstract, while every inclusion required the full text (Figure~\ref{fig:flow}); an early exclusion can therefore miss a relevant paper, and the recall check bounds this risk only for the papers we knew in advance.} Extraction was drafted from full text and the reported numbers were re-checked against the papers, but some versions read were preprints rather than the published version; we mark these in the data.

\paragraph{External validity.} The map reflects the main proceedings of ICSE, FSE and ASE from 2024 to 2026, without co-located workshops \new{that publish their own proceedings}, where much legacy and migration work appears\new{, and without other venues such as ICSME, SANER and ESEM, where one closely related study appeared~\cite{polyhuman}}; this area also moves fast on arXiv, so the newest work may be missing. Our conclusions are limited to practice at these venues. \new{The corpus also mixes full papers with \new{seven short ones (NIER, posters, a demonstration and an Ideas, Visions and Reflections paper)}, which describe their evaluation in less detail and may therefore understate the oracles they use.} \cmt{Claude}{[R32] Journals are missing from this list: TSE, TOSEM and EMSE papers count only when presented as journal-first. Name them as a limitation, since many evaluation-heavy migration studies appear there.}

\begin{added}
\paragraph{The pilot.} The pilot shows that oracles can disagree on one fixed set of translations; it does not estimate how often they disagree in general, nor does it rank them. It uses one translator and one judge, both small models quantized to 4 bits, a single run at temperature~0, one direction (Python to Java) and 164 function-level problems, far smaller than the repository-level migrations in the map. A larger judge, another prompt, other language pairs or several samples per task could change every rate in Table~\ref{tab:pilot-oracles}; in particular, the judge's errors are those of an 8B model. HumanEval-X has been public for several years, so both models may have seen its problems or solutions during training, which could raise both the test pass rate and the CodeBLEU scores. The reference is itself imperfect: we compare with the benchmark tests rather than running the Python originals on generated inputs, and these tests miss up to 16\% of the seeded faults (Section~\ref{sec:pilot-design}), so some translations counted as correct may not be equivalent. Finally, only 32 translations fail the tests, so the false-accept rates have wide 95\% Wilson intervals that overlap: 31--64\% for compilation, 26--58\% for CodeBLEU at 0.6 and 34--66\% for the judge. Likewise, the judge's higher agreement with the tests than CodeBLEU at 0.6 (73.8\% against 70.7\%) amounts to five tasks.
\end{added}
